# Expert lineup playbook

Use these rules as tie-breakers and risk controls. They encode veteran fantasy-football judgement beyond generic player rankings.

## 1. Start from minutes, then upside

- A strong player expected to start and play 75-90 minutes is usually preferable to a flashy rotation risk.
- A bench player can still be a valid upside start only when he is highly likely to enter, has a meaningful attacking role, and the bench provides safe substitution coverage.
- Treat a player returning from injury differently from a fully fit starter even if listed in the probable XI.

## 2. Read the real tactical role

- Value where the player actually receives the ball: box presence, half-spaces, width, deep buildup, set-piece zones.
- A nominal midfielder playing as a second striker or advanced winger can outrank a better-known midfielder used deeper.
- Full-backs/wing-backs with sustained attacking height deserve extra consideration; center-backs need set-piece threat or strong vote reliability to compensate.
- Track role changes after coaching changes or formation switches before relying on season averages.

## 3. Weight set pieces correctly

- Penalty duty is a major edge but verify the current hierarchy.
- Direct free kicks and corners matter more when the player also has reliable minutes.
- Do not overvalue nominal set-piece takers who are frequently substituted before late-game opportunities.

## 4. Model the match, not just the opponent name

Estimate the likely game script:

- favorite expected to dominate possession;
- underdog likely to defend deep and counter;
- open transition match;
- low-event tactical match;
- team likely to chase the game.

Then ask which player's role benefits from that script. A counterattacking winger can be preferable away to a stronger side; a possession-dependent playmaker can lose value when his team is likely to be pinned back.

## 5. Use team context and schedule congestion

- Check European matches, cup games, travel and short rest.
- Learn coach-specific rotation behavior: some rotate before Europe, others after Europe, others rarely touch the spine of the team.
- Do not assume "played in Europe = benched"; use actual historical rotation patterns and press-conference clues.
- New coaches and tactical experiments increase uncertainty until roles stabilize.

## 6. Separate repeatable form from noise

Prefer repeatable signals:

- expected minutes;
- shots and shots in the box;
- xG/xA or quality chances;
- touches in dangerous areas;
- key passes/chances created;
- set-piece role;
- stable tactical position.

Discount:

- one recent goal from a low-probability shot;
- one clean sheet despite heavy chances conceded;
- fantasy points driven by penalties won, own goals or rare events;
- short hot/cold streaks without role change.

## 7. Manage floor and ceiling deliberately

Default to expected value, not maximum theoretical ceiling.

Favor floor when:

- the alternative has serious no-vote risk;
- substitutions are limited;
- several starters in the XI are already uncertain;
- a structural bonus such as the defensive modifier rewards reliable votes.

Allow more ceiling when:

- the safe alternatives are genuinely weak;
- bench coverage is strong;
- the user's matchup situation explicitly calls for variance;
- the upside comes from a real role advantage, not wishful thinking.

## 8. Optimize risk across the whole XI

- Avoid stacking too many doubtful/rotation-prone players unless coverage is excellent.
- If selecting a risky starter, place the best reliable same-role substitute early enough on the bench according to league rules.
- Consider correlated downside: several defenders/goalkeeper from the same weak defensive matchup can fail together.
- Correlation is not automatically bad; a strong goalkeeper-defender unit can be valuable with a defensive modifier.

## 9. Defensive modifier logic

When a modifier exists, optimize the unit:

- goalkeeper matchup and save/goal-concession profile;
- defender vote reliability;
- clean-sheet/low-concession probability;
- card and error risk;
- starter certainty;
- attacking upside only after structural reliability.

Do not abandon a strong four-defender modifier setup merely to add a marginal attacker. Compare the expected value of the structural bonus against the offensive alternative.

## 10. Position-specific veteran heuristics

### Goalkeepers
- Prefer clean-sheet probability and defensive environment, but account for scoring rules that reward saves or punish goals heavily.
- Home advantage matters, but team defensive quality and opponent attack matter more than venue alone.

### Defenders
- For pure lineup choice, separate reliable-vote defenders from bonus hunters.
- Set-piece center-backs gain value against teams weak on aerial dead balls.
- Attacking wing-backs gain value when their team is expected to control territory.

### Midfielders
- Favor players with advanced role, set pieces, box entries and reliable minutes.
- Beware famous names used as deep registas or defensive midfielders when fantasy scoring rewards goals/assists heavily.

### Forwards
- Minutes and penalty-box role dominate.
- A striker can remain startable after a scoring drought if chances and minutes remain strong.
- A recent scorer can remain benchable if his role/minutes are poor and the goal was not repeatable.

## 11. Bench order is part of the decision

- Bench order should protect the weakest points of the starting XI, not simply rank substitutes by fame.
- Put reliable vote coverage behind uncertain starters when rules permit role-compatible replacement.
- If two bench players have similar vote probability, prefer the one with greater upside.

## 12. Common expert traps to avoid

- Chasing last week's bonus.
- Starting a star solely because he is a star.
- Treating probable-lineup percentages as exact probabilities.
- Ignoring tactical role changes.
- Ignoring coach rotation history.
- Overweighting home/away without matchup context.
- Using raw season totals without minutes normalization.
- Choosing every player independently instead of optimizing the XI and bench as a system.
- Hiding uncertainty. A strong manager knows which decisions are robust and which are coin flips.
